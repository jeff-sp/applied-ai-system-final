# Mood notes

Background on every mood label used in `data/songs.csv`. Each section describes what
the mood means musically, which audio features tend to correlate with it, which
genres carry it most reliably, and which everyday phrasings point at it.

Mood is the label listeners reach for most often and describe least precisely.
"Something chill" and "something relaxed" arrive as the same request but are stored
as different labels; these notes exist so retrieval can bridge the gap.

## Happy

Musically, happiness reads as major tonality, a bright timbral balance, and a rhythm
that lands squarely on the beat without dragging. Valence is the defining feature —
usually above 0.7 — and it tends to travel with high danceability, because music
that makes people move is usually music that reads as cheerful.

Energy is generally moderate to high, 0.6 to 0.9, though a gentle acoustic song can
be unmistakably happy at 0.4. Tempos cluster between 100 and 130 BPM. The genres
that carry it most reliably are pop, ska, funk, and reggae; it appears least often
in ambient and metal.

Listeners request it as "upbeat", "feel-good", "something to lift me", or by
occasion — mornings, cleaning, getting ready. It is the default request when
someone wants music to change how they feel rather than match it.

## Energetic

Energetic is about drive rather than emotional colour. The marker is sustained
rhythmic intensity: a strong pulse, forward momentum, and an arrangement that keeps
pushing rather than settling. Energy sits high, usually above 0.75, and tempo
typically exceeds 120 BPM.

Unlike happy, valence is not reliably high. Energetic music can be aggressive,
anxious, or triumphant — what unites it is that it raises the listener's arousal
level. Edm, metal, ska, and up-tempo rock are the strongest carriers, with
high-tempo hip hop and jazz fusion close behind.

The everyday phrasings are unmistakable: "for the gym", "workout", "hype me up",
"pump-up", "something to run to", "keep me awake". It is the mood most often
requested for a physical task rather than an emotional state.

## Nostalgic

Nostalgia is a production quality as much as a compositional one. Period-specific
timbres do most of the work — analogue synthesisers, gated reverb, tape saturation,
a particular kind of chorus-drenched guitar. The music signals a decade, and the
decade supplies the feeling.

Energy is usually moderate, 0.45 to 0.8, with mid-range tempos around 95 to 125 BPM.
Valence is characteristically ambivalent: warm on the surface with something wistful
underneath, which is exactly what distinguishes nostalgic from happy. City pop and
synthwave are its clearest homes, with folk and indie pop carrying a quieter version.

Requests arrive as "80s", "retro", "throwback", "old-school", or through scenes that
imply memory — late-night drives, summers that have already ended.

## Intense

Intensity is pressure sustained over time. The arrangement is dense, dynamics stay
compressed near the top, and there is little relief — where energetic music
propels, intense music bears down. Energy runs 0.75 to 0.95 and the low end is
usually prominent.

Valence is typically low to middling. The genres that carry it best are metal, hard
rock, the darker end of edm, and dramatic orchestral classical. Tempo is less
diagnostic than density: a slow, heavy track can be far more intense than a fast,
light one.

Listeners ask for it as "heavy", "hard", "something with weight", or by task —
heavy lifting, deep concentration through force, driving fast. It overlaps with
angry but is broader: intense music need not be hostile, only relentless.

## Confident

Confidence in music is swagger — a groove so assured it does not hurry. The pocket
is deep, the bass leads, and the arrangement leaves space rather than filling every
gap. That restraint is the signal: the music is not trying to convince anyone.

Energy is moderate to high, 0.6 to 0.85, with tempos in a comfortable 95 to 120 BPM
range where a groove can breathe. Danceability is high and valence runs warm. Funk
is the definitive carrier, with hip hop, r&b, and city pop close behind.

Requests come as "something with swagger", "makes me feel like the main character",
"getting ready to go out", or "walking music". It is the mood most tied to
self-presentation rather than to a private emotional state.

## Hopeful

Hopeful music is built on ascent. Melodies rise, harmonic progressions resolve
upward, and arrangements frequently grow — an arrangement that starts sparse and
accumulates is doing the emotional work. The feeling is forward-looking rather than
presently joyful.

Energy is moderate, 0.45 to 0.75, with tempos between 90 and 130 BPM. Valence is
positive but usually less emphatic than happy, because hope implies something not
yet resolved. Indie pop, folk, classical, and the warmer end of rock carry it well.

Listeners phrase it as "uplifting", "something optimistic", "music for starting
over", or attach it to transitions — new jobs, new cities, recovering from
something. It is a common request when the listener wants encouragement rather than
distraction.

## Moody

Moody is atmosphere over statement. The register is dim rather than dark: minor-ish
harmony, reverb-heavy space, restrained dynamics, and vocals that sit back in the
mix. Nothing resolves emphatically, and that unresolved quality is the point.

Energy is moderate, 0.4 to 0.7 — lower than intense, higher than melancholy. Valence
is low but not desolate. Indie rock, indie pop, synthwave, and the darker end of r&b
carry it most reliably, with ambient supplying an instrumental version.

Requests arrive as "something moody", "for a rainy day", "late-night", or "something
that matches how I feel". It is the mood listeners choose when they want music to
accompany a state rather than change it.

## Melancholy

Melancholy is sadness with the volume down. Slow tempos, sparse arrangements, minor
tonality, and considerable space between elements. Where moody is atmospheric,
melancholy is specific — usually a voice and a small number of instruments, close
enough to feel confessional.

Energy is low, 0.2 to 0.45, with tempos between 60 and 95 BPM. Valence is at the
bottom of the range, and acousticness often runs high because the arrangements are
small. Folk, classical, indie pop, and slow r&b are its natural homes.

Listeners ask for it as "sad", "heartbreak", "something for crying", or "quiet". It
is worth honouring literally: someone requesting sad music generally wants their
feeling met, not corrected.

## Romantic

Romantic music is intimate in scale and warm in timbre. Tempos are unhurried, the
vocal is close-mixed and expressive, and the harmony is rich — extended chords,
gentle suspensions, resolutions that arrive slowly. The arrangement leaves room for
phrasing.

Energy is low to moderate, 0.3 to 0.6, with tempos between 60 and 100 BPM. Valence
runs warm without being bright, and danceability is moderate — the motion it invites
is slow. R&b is the strongest carrier, with jazz ballads, soul-inflected pop, and
quiet folk close behind.

Requests come as "date night", "dinner", "something slow", "for two people". It is
one of the few moods that is nearly always tied to a social situation rather than a
solitary one.

## Chill

Chill is low-stakes ease. There is a pulse and it is steady, but nothing in the
arrangement demands anything — no dramatic arrivals, no wide dynamic swings, no
lyrical urgency. It is background music that is pleasant enough to foreground if you
choose to.

Energy is low, 0.25 to 0.5, with tempos in a gentle 70 to 100 BPM range. Valence is
mildly positive. Lofi is the definitive carrier, with reggae, downtempo r&b, and the
softer end of indie pop alongside it.

Listeners phrase it as "chill", "something in the background", "nothing intense",
"study music", "hanging out". It sits alongside relaxed and is frequently requested
interchangeably, though chill implies company and relaxed implies rest.

## Relaxed

Relaxed music actively lowers arousal. Tempos are slow, dynamics are narrow, timbres
are soft, and there is often no percussion at all. Where chill accompanies an
activity, relaxed is aimed at doing less — it is closer to rest than to background.

Energy is at the low end, 0.2 to 0.4, with tempos below 90 BPM or absent entirely.
Acousticness is often high. Ambient, folk, quiet classical, and the slowest end of
lofi and reggae carry it best.

Requests arrive as "something calming", "to unwind", "wind-down", "before bed",
"help me stop thinking". The distinction from chill matters in practice: a listener
asking to unwind after a hard day usually does not want a beat, even a gentle one.

## Focused

Focused music is engineered to be ignored. The defining properties are negative:
no lyrics competing for language processing, no dynamic surprises, no structural
events that pull attention. Repetition is desirable and predictability is the
feature.

Energy is low to moderate, 0.3 to 0.6, with steady tempos usually between 70 and 110
BPM. Consistency matters more than any specific value — a track that stays where it
started is better than one that develops. Ambient, lofi, instrumental classical, and
minimal synthwave are the reliable carriers.

Listeners ask for it as "study music", "for coding", "deep work", "concentration",
"something to write to". It is the clearest case where the listener's real
requirement is functional, and where a genuinely great song would be the wrong
answer.

## Angry

Anger in music is aggression given form. Distortion, percussive attack, vocal
delivery pushed toward the top of its range, and a rhythmic insistence that refuses
to relent. The arrangement is dense and the dynamic range is compressed hard.

Energy is at the maximum, 0.8 to 0.98, with tempos usually above 140 BPM. Valence is
at the bottom. Metal is the primary carrier, with hardcore-adjacent rock and the
most aggressive hip hop alongside it.

Requests come as "angry", "something to scream to", "rage", "hard workout", or in
response to a specific frustration. Like melancholy, it is best honoured literally —
the function is catharsis, and offering something calming instead misreads what is
being asked for.
