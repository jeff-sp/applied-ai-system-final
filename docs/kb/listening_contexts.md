# Listening context notes

Most real requests name a situation rather than a genre. "Something for the gym",
"music to study to", "we're cooking dinner" — the listener knows what they are
doing and expects the system to work out what that implies.

Each section below describes what a context actually demands, which genres and moods
serve it, and where the obvious answer is wrong. This is the file that does the most
work translating everyday phrasing into catalog labels.

## Studying and deep focus

The requirement here is negative: music that does not compete for attention. Lyrics
are the main problem, because language processing collides directly with reading and
writing. Structural events are the second problem — a key change or a dramatic entry
pulls focus even when the listener is not consciously listening.

What works is instrumental, repetitive, and dynamically flat. Lofi is the default
answer and deserves to be. Ambient works when even a gentle beat is intrusive.
Instrumental classical and minimal synthwave both work, with the caveat that
classical's wide dynamic range can be disruptive in a quiet room.

Target energy sits around 0.25 to 0.45, moods are focused, chill, or relaxed. The
counterintuitive rule: a genuinely excellent, engaging song is the wrong answer.
Someone asking for study music is asking not to notice the music.

## Workout and the gym

Exercise music is a tempo tool. A steady pulse above roughly 130 BPM helps sustain
cadence, and high perceived energy raises arousal and lowers perceived effort. The
functional requirement is consistency: a track that drops to half-time mid-set works
against the listener.

Edm is the most reliable answer, engineered almost exactly for this. Metal serves
listeners who want intensity from a guitar band. High-tempo hip hop is the standard
choice for lifting, where the confidence in the delivery matters as much as the
tempo. Ska is an underrated option for cardio.

Target energy is 0.8 and above, moods are energetic, intense, angry, or confident.
The common failure is offering something merely upbeat — happy pop at 0.65 energy
does not sustain a run.

## Late-night driving

Driving after dark is the context with the strongest aesthetic consensus. The music
should be propulsive without being frantic — a steady forward motion matching the
road rather than a series of peaks and drops. Space in the arrangement matters,
since the setting supplies its own atmosphere.

Synthwave and city pop are the definitive answers, both built around exactly this
image. Moody indie rock works for a less glamorous version, and the darker end of
r&b works when the drive is a wind-down rather than a departure.

Target energy is 0.55 to 0.8, moods are nostalgic, moody, or confident. Tempo
matters less than steadiness; anything that keeps stopping and restarting breaks the
feeling the context depends on.

## Commuting

The commute is the compromise context. The environment is noisy, so quiet and
dynamic material disappears entirely. Attention is partly occupied, so demanding
music is frustrating. And the emotional job varies by direction — the morning
commute usually wants activation, the evening one usually wants decompression.

Morning: pop, funk, up-tempo hip hop, ska, at 0.65 to 0.85 energy with happy,
energetic, or confident moods. Evening: r&b, indie pop, reggae, at 0.4 to 0.6 with
chill or relaxed moods.

The practical constraint is compression. Music with a narrow dynamic range survives
traffic and train noise; classical and ambient largely do not, however well they suit
the mood.

## Dinner and hosting

Music at a gathering has one job: fill silence without competing with conversation.
It needs to be present enough that a lull is not awkward and unobtrusive enough that
nobody raises their voice over it. Familiarity helps — recognisable music invites
comment, which is a feature at a party and a bug at a dinner.

Jazz is the traditional answer for good reason: harmonically rich, rhythmically
gentle, and it reads as considered. Reggae works for a warmer and more casual
gathering. Soul-inflected r&b and city pop both sit well. Bossa-adjacent material
within jazz is close to purpose-built.

Target energy is 0.35 to 0.6, moods are chill, relaxed, romantic, or happy. Avoid
anything with wide dynamic swings or aggressive low end — both force people to
adjust their speaking volume.

## Falling asleep

Sleep music should decelerate. Slow tempos below 70 BPM, soft timbres, minimal
percussion, and — most importantly — no structural surprises. A single unexpected
entrance can undo twenty minutes of settling.

Ambient is the primary answer, since the absence of a pulse is an advantage rather
than a limitation. Slow classical works, with the caveat about dynamic range applying
doubly here. The quietest end of lofi works for listeners who find pure ambient
uncomfortably empty.

Target energy is 0.2 to 0.35, moods are relaxed or chill. Lyrics are best avoided
entirely. This is the context where getting it wrong is most consequential, so
consistency should be preferred over interest in every case.

## Heartbreak

The instinct to cheer someone up is usually wrong. Music that matches a difficult
feeling tends to help more than music that contradicts it — being met is more useful
than being distracted, at least at first. A listener asking for sad music has
generally already worked out what they need.

Melancholy folk, slow r&b, quiet indie pop, and solo classical all serve this well.
Lyrical specificity is valuable here in a way it is not elsewhere; the words are
doing real work.

Target energy is 0.2 to 0.45, moods are melancholy, moody, or romantic. The
transition out, when the listener asks for it, usually runs through hopeful rather
than happy — ascent is easier to accept than brightness.

## Cleaning and chores

Chores need momentum and tolerate almost anything else. The environment is noisy,
attention is minimal, and the music's job is to make repetitive physical work feel
shorter. Familiarity and singability both help.

Pop, funk, ska, and up-tempo reggae all work. High-energy hip hop works. This is one
of the few contexts where an obvious, unsubtle choice is the correct one — the
listener is not evaluating the music.

Target energy is 0.7 to 0.9, moods are happy, energetic, or confident. The only real
failure mode is anything too quiet or too slow to survive a vacuum cleaner.

## Coding and creative work

Distinct from studying, because the attention pattern differs. Programming and
similar work alternates between deep concentration and mechanical execution, and the
music that suits each phase is different. Many people also report that familiar
music works better than new music, since novelty pulls attention.

Lofi and ambient serve the concentration phases. Minimal synthwave and instrumental
electronic material work well for mechanical stretches, where a steadier pulse helps
maintain pace. Instrumental hip hop sits usefully between the two.

Target energy is 0.3 to 0.6, moods are focused, chill, or moody. Lyrics are a problem
specifically when writing prose or reading documentation, and less of a problem
during mechanical work — which is why a listener's answer may change mid-session.

## Road trips

Long daytime driving with company is close to the opposite of the late-night drive.
It is social, it is long, and it wants variety rather than a single sustained mood.
Singability matters because people will sing.

Country is close to purpose-built for it. Rock, pop, and indie rock all work.
Anything sufficiently familiar to a car full of people beats anything more
interesting that only one person knows.

Target energy is 0.55 to 0.85, moods are happy, hopeful, nostalgic, or confident. The
one real constraint is duration: a road trip needs range, so a request for it is
better served by breadth across several genres than by depth in one.
