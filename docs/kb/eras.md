# Era notes

Background on the periods and scenes the catalog draws from. Listeners often ask for
music by decade rather than by genre — "something 80s", "90s throwback" — and these
notes connect those requests to the genres and moods that actually carry them.

Each section covers what the period sounded like, why it sounded that way, and which
catalog genres belong to it.

## Sixties and seventies soul and funk

The rhythm section became the composition. Where earlier popular music treated the
groove as accompaniment to a melody, soul and then funk inverted the priority: James
Brown's bands built entire records on a single vamp, with horns punctuating and the
bass carrying the melodic interest. Recording was live to tape, whole bands playing
together in a room, so the push and pull between players is audible in a way that
later multitracked production loses.

The technological context matters. Analogue tape saturated pleasantly when driven
hard, mixing consoles imposed a warm compression, and the practical limit on track
count forced arrangement decisions to be made before recording rather than after.
The result is music with enormous physical presence and very little clutter.

This period is the ancestor of a great deal that follows. Hip hop sampled it
extensively; r&b descends from it directly; city pop's session musicians studied it
closely. In the catalog it appears as funk, and as the harmonic vocabulary
underneath much of r&b.

## Seventies and eighties reggae and ska

Jamaican music exported two rhythmic ideas that reshaped popular music elsewhere.
Ska came first — fast, horn-driven, built on an offbeat guitar chop — and reggae
followed by slowing that same chop down and pushing the bass forward until it became
the melodic centre. The innovation was as much technical as musical: dub producers
treated the mixing desk as an instrument, dropping elements in and out and drenching
the results in spring reverb and tape delay.

That studio experimentation is why the period still sounds modern. The idea that a
recording is raw material to be remixed rather than a fixed document originated here
and now underpins essentially all electronic music.

The two-tone revival carried ska into British popular music at the end of the
seventies, fusing it with punk energy. In the catalog this period appears as reggae
and ska, and its rhythmic influence is audible in parts of hip hop and pop.

## Eighties city pop and synthwave

Japan's economic boom produced city pop: extraordinarily well-funded studio
recordings by musicians with deep jazz and funk training, aimed at an urban audience
with new cars and new stereos. The music is glossy, harmonically adventurous, and
optimistic in a way that is inseparable from the moment that produced it. Extended
chords and abrupt key changes are routine, delivered with a lightness that hides how
difficult the writing is.

Synthwave is the same decade heard from a distance and reconstructed. It emerged
decades later from artists who knew the eighties through film soundtracks and video
games rather than lived experience, which is why it emphasises the era's most
cinematic gestures — gated snares, arpeggiated basslines, wide analogue pads.

The pairing is instructive: one is authentic period nostalgia, the other is
deliberate pastiche, and listeners often want them interchangeably. Both are strong
answers to a request for night driving, and both carry the nostalgic mood in the
catalog.

## Nineties hip hop

Sampling matured into a compositional practice. Producers dug through soul, jazz, and
funk records for a four-bar loop, chopped it, layered drums beneath it, and built a
track from material that already carried decades of feeling. The legal environment
tightened over the decade, which pushed production toward fewer and more transformed
samples, but the aesthetic was established: dusty, warm, rhythmically loose.

The vocal tradition matured alongside it. Rapping grew more rhythmically intricate
and lyrically dense, treating the voice as a percussion instrument that also carried
narrative — a dual role no other popular vocal tradition demands.

The period's influence on the catalog is broad. Hip hop obviously, but also lofi,
which is essentially the instrumental, texture-forward end of nineties boom-bap
production isolated and slowed down for background listening.

## Two-thousands indie

Cheap recording technology changed who could make records. A laptop and an interface
replaced a studio budget, and a generation of bands recorded themselves — often
badly, sometimes brilliantly, and almost always with a texture that distinguished
them from major-label production. Distribution followed: blogs and file sharing
routed around radio entirely.

The aesthetic that emerged prized character over competence. A thin drum sound, an
uncertain vocal, or a guitar tone nobody would have approved in a professional
session became markers of authenticity rather than defects.

In the catalog this period is indie rock and indie pop. Its emotional register — 
ambivalent, wry, unresolved — is why those genres carry the moody and melancholy
labels far more often than mainstream rock and pop do.

## The internet lofi era

Lofi hip hop became a genre defined by its use case rather than its origin. Endless
streaming radio, animated study-companion loops, and playlists titled by activity
rather than artist turned it into functional music at a scale nothing else matches.
Individual tracks are close to interchangeable by design; the product is the stream.

Musically it descends from nineties sampling, but the priorities inverted. Where
boom-bap wanted a head-nod hard enough to command attention, lofi wants to be
pleasant and forgettable. Loops repeat with minimal variation, arrivals are avoided,
and the vinyl crackle that was once an artefact is now added deliberately.

It is the clearest case in the catalog of music optimised to be ignored — and it
sits alongside ambient as the answer to focus and study requests.

## The classical canon

Several centuries collapse into a single catalog label, which is worth stating
plainly. Baroque counterpoint, Classical-period formal clarity, Romantic emotional
expansion, and twentieth-century minimalism are not one thing, and a listener asking
for "classical" may want any of them.

What the label reliably signals is acoustic instrumentation, composed rather than
improvised material, and structures that develop over minutes rather than returning
to a hook every thirty seconds. Dynamic range is far wider than in produced popular
music, which is why classical recordings can be difficult as background listening in
a noisy room.

Within the catalog, classical serves two quite different requests: focused work,
where slow instrumental material excels, and attentive listening, where the
composition itself is the object. Ambient and folk are the nearest neighbours for
the first use, and jazz for the second.
