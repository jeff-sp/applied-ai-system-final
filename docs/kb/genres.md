# Genre notes

Background on every genre present in `data/songs.csv`. Each section describes what
the genre sounds like, the energy and tempo range it usually occupies, what a
listener is typically after when they ask for it, and which neighbouring genres
make reasonable substitutes when the catalog is thin.

These notes exist to bridge everyday language to the catalog's categorical labels.
A listener rarely asks for "genre: edm, energy: 0.85" — they ask for something to
run to. The prose below is what lets a retrieval system connect the two.

## Jazz

Jazz is built on improvisation over a flexible harmonic frame. A standard head is
stated, soloists take turns stretching it, and the rhythm section reacts in real
time rather than repeating a fixed pattern. That conversational quality is the
thing people mean when they call a recording "alive" — no two performances of the
same tune land identically, and the interplay between players carries as much
information as the melody.

The energy range is unusually wide. Solo piano ballads and late-night quartet
sessions sit low, around 0.25 to 0.45, with tempos loitering between 60 and 90
BPM. Hard bop, big band, and jazz fusion push well past 0.75 and can run past 200
BPM on an up-tempo burner. This spread is why "jazz" alone is a weak request:
someone asking for energetic jazz and someone asking for dinner jazz want almost
opposite recordings.

Listeners reach for jazz in two distinct modes. One is attentive listening, where
the improvisation is the point. The other is atmosphere — jazz as a warm,
harmonically rich backdrop that fills a room without demanding attention. Adjacent
choices are funk when the groove matters more than the harmony, r&b when the
listener wants vocals, and classical when they want acoustic sophistication
without the improvisation.

## Pop

Pop is the craft of maximum immediacy. Structures are tight and familiar, hooks
arrive early and often, production is polished until every element sits exactly
where the ear expects it. The genre absorbs whatever else is happening in music
and files off the difficult edges, which is why pop from different decades can
sound wildly different while still being unmistakably pop.

Energy usually sits between 0.6 and 0.9 with tempos clustering in the 100 to 130
BPM range — fast enough to feel forward-moving, slow enough to sing along to.
Valence tends to run high; even melancholy pop is usually delivered with a bright,
danceable arrangement underneath. Danceability is reliably high because the genre
is engineered for it.

People ask for pop when they want a guaranteed lift with no effort required:
mornings, cleaning, parties, anything where music is the accompaniment to motion.
Adjacent options are indie pop for the same melodic instinct with rougher edges,
edm when the request skews harder toward dancing, and r&b when the listener wants
groove and vocal performance over hooks.

## Lofi

Lofi is defined by texture rather than composition. Muted drums, warm and slightly
detuned keys, vinyl crackle, tape hiss, a deliberate softness in the high end. The
imperfection is the aesthetic — the genre borrows the sound of degraded media on
purpose, and the resulting haze is what makes it recede so comfortably into the
background.

Energy is consistently low, roughly 0.2 to 0.4, with tempos in a narrow 70 to 90
BPM band. Acousticness varies but the perceived warmth is high regardless.
Crucially, lofi tracks are structurally uneventful: loops repeat with minimal
variation and there is rarely a dramatic arrival. That predictability is a feature.
Music that surprises you pulls attention, and attention is exactly what a lofi
listener is trying to keep elsewhere.

The request is almost always functional — studying, working, reading, falling
asleep. Someone asking for lofi is usually asking for the absence of distraction
rather than for a particular sound. Adjacent choices are ambient when even the beat
is too much, and the chill end of hip hop when the listener still wants rhythm.

## Rock

Rock is guitar, bass, and drums pushed to fill a room. The core is the riff and the
backbeat, with distortion supplying weight and a vocal delivering attitude over the
top. Dynamics matter enormously: the genre's characteristic move is the drop into a
quiet verse so the chorus can hit harder by contrast.

Energy typically runs 0.65 to 0.9, tempos 110 to 160 BPM. It is louder and more
physically insistent than pop but generally less relentless than metal, and it
usually keeps a melodic vocal at the centre rather than burying it. Acousticness is
low by definition; the electric guitar tone is the genre's signature.

Listeners want rock for momentum and catharsis — driving, working out, or simply
wanting something with more force than the day is providing. Adjacent choices are
indie rock for the same instrumentation with less polish and more idiosyncrasy,
metal when the request is for more aggression, and funk when the listener wants
groove rather than power.

## Ambient

Ambient music treats sound as environment. There is often no beat, no clear
melodic line, and no structure in the conventional sense — instead, slowly evolving
textures, sustained tones, and space. Brian Eno's framing still describes it best:
it should reward attention without requiring it.

Energy is the lowest in the catalog, often below 0.3, and tempo is frequently
meaningless because there is no pulse to measure. Acousticness varies wildly since
the genre spans field recordings, processed instruments, and pure synthesis. What
unites it is the absence of events: nothing arrives to demand a reaction.

The use case is almost entirely functional — deep focus, meditation, sleep, or
covering an unpleasant acoustic environment. It is the correct answer when a
listener says even lofi is too busy or when a beat itself is the distraction.
Adjacent choices are lofi when a gentle pulse is welcome, classical for slow
acoustic pieces, and the sparser end of synthwave for something atmospheric with
more shape.

## Synthwave

Synthwave reconstructs an imagined 1980s: analogue synthesiser pads, gated reverb
on the snare, arpeggiated basslines, and lead tones borrowed from film scores of
the era. It is deliberately nostalgic for a decade most of its listeners did not
experience, which gives it a distinctive quality of memory without a memory.

Energy typically sits 0.5 to 0.8 with tempos around 100 to 130 BPM. The rhythmic
feel is steady and propulsive rather than explosive — it drives forward at a
constant rate, which is precisely why it works so well as driving music.
Acousticness is near zero; this is an unapologetically electronic genre.

The strongest association is night: neon, motorways, long drives after dark.
Listeners also use it for focused work, since it is rhythmically consistent and
usually instrumental. Adjacent choices are city pop for warmer, more organic
80s nostalgia, edm for higher-intensity electronic music, and ambient for the
atmospheric end without the beat.

## Indie pop

Indie pop keeps pop's melodic instinct but trades the gloss for texture and space.
Arrangements leave gaps, vocals are often mixed softer or more conversationally,
and production choices favour character over perfection — a slightly out-of-tune
guitar or an obviously room-recorded drum kit is a feature.

Energy lands in a moderate band, roughly 0.4 to 0.7, with tempos across a wide 90
to 140 BPM range. Valence is noticeably more variable than mainstream pop: indie
pop is comfortable being wistful, ambivalent, or bittersweet in a way that chart
pop usually smooths away. Danceability is moderate rather than engineered.

Listeners reach for it when they want melody with some emotional complexity, or
music that suits a specific mood rather than lifting them out of it. It is a
reliable middle setting — present enough to enjoy, restrained enough to work
alongside. Adjacent choices are indie rock for more guitar and drive, pop for a
brighter and more immediate version, and folk for the acoustic end.

## Reggae

Reggae is organised around the offbeat. The guitar or keyboard chops on the upbeat
while the bass carries the melodic weight, producing the genre's characteristic
loping, unhurried motion. The drums sit back, often emphasising the third beat, and
the whole arrangement feels like it is leaning rather than pushing.

Energy is moderate, usually 0.4 to 0.65, with tempos in a relaxed 60 to 90 BPM
band. Valence tends to run warm even when the lyrics are political or grieving —
the rhythmic feel itself is unhurried and buoyant. Danceability is high but the
dancing it invites is loose rather than energetic.

Listeners want reggae for warmth and ease: sunshine, cooking, social gatherings
that should stay relaxed. Adjacent choices are ska for the same rhythmic DNA played
much faster and brighter, funk for a harder groove, and r&b when the request skews
toward vocals and smoothness.

## Hip hop

Hip hop is built on the beat and the voice. A looped or programmed rhythm provides
the frame, and the rapped vocal carries rhythm, melody, and meaning simultaneously.
Production ranges from dusty sampled soul to stark, sub-heavy minimalism, but the
low end is almost always the foundation.

Energy spans a wide range: introspective, jazz-sampling tracks sit near 0.4, while
club and drill records push past 0.85. Tempos are similarly spread, from 70 to 160
BPM, though the perceived tempo is often half or double the measured one because of
how the drums are subdivided. Danceability is generally high.

Listeners reach for hip hop for confidence and momentum — workouts, commutes,
getting into a task — or, at the mellower end, for atmosphere while working.
Adjacent choices are r&b when the listener wants sung vocals over the same rhythmic
sensibility, funk for the live-band ancestor of many of its samples, and lofi for
the instrumental, beat-only end.

## Classical

Classical covers several centuries of Western composed music, so any generalisation
is rough. What the pieces share is that they are composed rather than improvised,
performed on acoustic instruments, and structured over longer arcs than popular
music — a movement develops material patiently rather than returning to a hook.

Acousticness is near the maximum by definition. Energy varies enormously: a solo
nocturne sits near 0.2 while a full orchestral finale exceeds 0.8. Tempo is likewise
unbounded. The distinguishing feature is dynamic range — classical recordings move
between very quiet and very loud, which is exactly why they can be difficult as
background music in a noisy room.

Listeners choose classical for focused work, for calm, or for attentive listening
where the composition itself is the object. Adjacent choices are ambient for slow
atmospheric material without the dynamic swings, folk for smaller-scale acoustic
music, and jazz for acoustic sophistication with improvisation.

## Edm

Edm is dance music engineered for a room and a sound system. Structure is
functional: a build accumulates tension through filters and rising elements, a drop
releases it, and the arrangement is arranged around that cycle. The kick is
foregrounded, the low end is deliberately physical, and repetition is a tool rather
than a limitation.

Energy is the highest band in the catalog, commonly 0.75 to 0.98, with tempos
concentrated between 120 and 140 BPM. Danceability is maximal by design.
Acousticness is essentially zero. Whatever else varies between subgenres, the
insistence of the pulse does not.

The request is nearly always for activation: workouts, parties, staying awake,
pushing through the last hour of something. Edm is a poor choice for concentration
precisely because it is built to command attention. Adjacent choices are synthwave
for electronic music at a lower intensity, pop for hooks with a dance floor
underneath, and metal for high-energy intensity from a guitar band instead.

## Country

Country is a storytelling tradition. The narrative in the lyric is the organising
principle, and the arrangement — acoustic guitar, pedal steel, fiddle, plain drum
kit — exists to carry it clearly. Vocals are mixed forward and delivered with
recognisable regional character rather than smoothed into neutrality.

Energy typically sits 0.4 to 0.75 with tempos between 90 and 140 BPM.
Acousticness runs higher than most contemporary genres even in polished modern
country, where electric guitars sit alongside pedal steel rather than replacing it.
Valence is genuinely bimodal: the genre is equally committed to celebration and to
heartbreak, often on the same record.

Listeners want country for narrative and for a sense of place — driving, working
outdoors, unwinding. Adjacent choices are folk for the quieter, more acoustic
version of the same storytelling impulse, rock when the request skews toward drive,
and r&b when the listener wants emotional vocal delivery in a different idiom.

## R&b

R&b centres the voice. Arrangements are built to frame a vocal performance: a
pocketed groove, lush harmonic support, and space deliberately left for melisma and
phrasing. Production is smooth and low-end-forward, with drums that sit deep in the
pocket rather than driving hard.

Energy is moderate, roughly 0.4 to 0.7, with tempos in a slow-to-mid 60 to 110 BPM
band. Danceability is high despite the moderate energy, because the groove is
precise even when it is unhurried. Valence skews warm, and the emotional register
leans intimate — desire, devotion, regret.

Listeners reach for r&b for evenings, intimacy, and unwinding without going fully
quiet. Adjacent choices are funk for the same rhythmic ancestry with more attack,
hip hop for the rhythmic sensibility with rapped rather than sung vocals, and jazz
when the listener wants harmonic richness without the contemporary production.

## Metal

Metal is intensity as a design goal. Heavily distorted guitars, double-kick drum
patterns, and vocals that range from soaring to guttural combine into something
built to overwhelm. Precision matters more than the volume suggests — the genre
prizes technical execution, and its rhythmic complexity is frequently
underestimated by people who only hear the distortion.

Energy is at the top of the range, generally 0.8 to 0.98, with tempos from 90 BPM
in doom-adjacent material to well past 180 in thrash. Acousticness is near zero.
Valence is typically low, though the emotional effect is often cathartic rather
than depressing — the aggression is a release, not a wallow.

Listeners choose metal for hard workouts, for anger with somewhere to go, and for
focus through sheer intensity. Adjacent choices are rock at lower aggression, edm
for comparable energy from electronics, and the intense end of indie rock. It is a
poor substitute for anything requesting calm.

## Folk

Folk is acoustic, direct, and built around song rather than production. Guitar or
banjo, an unadorned vocal, sometimes fiddle or upright bass, recorded with enough
room sound that the performance feels like it happened in a place. The tradition
prizes lyrical craft and melodic durability — songs that survive being sung by
someone else with one instrument.

Energy is generally low to moderate, 0.25 to 0.55, with tempos between 70 and 120
BPM. Acousticness is high, usually above 0.6 and often far higher. Valence spans
the full range, since the tradition covers both celebration and lament, frequently
with the same restrained delivery.

Listeners want folk for calm, for lyrical attention, or for a sense of warmth and
proximity. It works well for reading, slow mornings, and long unhurried drives.
Adjacent choices are country for the same storytelling with a fuller arrangement,
indie pop for melodic material at moderate energy, and classical for acoustic music
without vocals.

## City pop

City pop is Japanese urban popular music of roughly the late 1970s and 1980s: highly
polished, harmonically sophisticated, and drawing on funk, soft rock, and jazz
fusion. Studio craft is extraordinary — session musicians of the first rank,
immaculate arrangements, and a production sheen that has aged remarkably well.

Energy commonly sits 0.6 to 0.85 with tempos in the 95 to 125 BPM range.
Danceability is high, valence runs warm and bright, and acousticness is low despite
the live instrumentation because everything is so cleanly produced. Extended chords
and unexpected key changes are routine, which is what distinguishes it from Western
pop of the same period.

The dominant association is motion at night: driving, city lights, a specific kind
of glamorous melancholy. Its second life came through internet rediscovery, so the
nostalgia it carries is often secondhand. Adjacent choices are synthwave for
electronic 80s nostalgia, funk for the groove without the polish, and r&b for
smooth vocal-led material.

## Funk

Funk is rhythm as the entire point. Everything serves the groove: a bass line that
is as melodic as it is percussive, guitar played as a percussion instrument, horns
punctuating rather than sustaining, and drums locked to an emphatic first beat. The
harmonic content is often deliberately static — a single vamp held for minutes so
the rhythm can do the work.

Energy runs 0.65 to 0.9 with tempos between 95 and 125 BPM. Danceability is at or
near the catalog maximum. Valence is high and the emotional register is confidence,
swagger, physical pleasure. Acousticness is low but the instrumentation is live,
which gives it a human push and pull that programmed music lacks.

Listeners want funk for movement and for confidence — parties, cooking, getting
ready to go out, or any moment that needs an injection of swagger. Adjacent choices
are r&b for the smoother descendant, hip hop which sampled it extensively, and ska
for a faster horn-driven groove.

## Ska

Ska is the fast, bright ancestor of reggae: the same offbeat guitar chop played at
double the speed, with horn sections carrying the melody and a walking bass driving
underneath. The overall effect is relentlessly buoyant — it is difficult to make ska
sound sad, and the tradition has largely not tried.

Energy is high, typically 0.7 to 0.9, with tempos from 130 to 180 BPM. Danceability
is very high and valence is near the top of the catalog. The horns are the
identifying feature; without them the arrangement reads as something else.

Listeners reach for ska for immediate, uncomplicated energy — parties, cleaning,
anything needing momentum without aggression. It is a good answer for someone who
wants high energy but finds edm too mechanical or metal too heavy. Adjacent choices
are reggae for the same DNA slowed and relaxed, funk for horn-driven groove at
moderate tempo, and rock for guitar-led drive.

## Indie rock

Indie rock is rock made outside the major-label machine, and the aesthetic follows
from the economics: rougher production, unconventional song structures, and a
willingness to be awkward or abrasive where mainstream rock would smooth things
over. Guitar tone is a signature rather than a default, ranging from jangling to
buried in fuzz.

Energy sits 0.5 to 0.8, a notch below mainstream rock, with tempos between 100 and
150 BPM. Valence is markedly more variable — the genre is comfortable with
ambivalence, irony, and unresolved endings. Acousticness is low but production is
often deliberately less compressed, leaving more dynamic movement.

Listeners want indie rock for guitar music with personality, for driving, and for
moods that mainstream rock's confidence does not fit. Adjacent choices are rock for
a bigger and more polished version, indie pop for the same sensibility with melody
foregrounded and energy lowered, and folk for the acoustic end of the same
songwriting tradition.
